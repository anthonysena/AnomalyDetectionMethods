# Learning Plausible Measurement Ranges Using Statistical and ML-Based Anomaly Detection

## Background

Measurement data in the OMOP Common Data Model (CDM) are central to many observational research use cases, including cohort definition, target trial emulation, and predictive modeling. However, measurement values in real-world data often exhibit noise, data entry errors, unit inconsistencies, and rare but valid observations. Traditional approaches to identifying implausible values rely on fixed thresholds or clinical reference ranges, which may not reflect the full variability observed in practice. Moreover, purely statistical outlier detection methods applied to the marginal distribution of values can fail to distinguish between biologically implausible values and rare but valid observations, particularly when distributions are skewed or heterogeneous across demographic subgroups.

To address these challenges, this work proposes a framework for learning biologically plausible minimum and maximum values for a given measurement concept and unit by combining multiple univariate statistical methods with context-aware anomaly detection approaches. The framework explicitly distinguishes between global plausibility, defined over the full population, and contextual plausibility, defined relative to demographic factors such as age and gender. By integrating these perspectives, the goal is to derive plausible ranges that are both statistically robust and clinically meaningful, while also identifying values that remain ambiguous or context-dependent.

## Proposed Methods

The analysis begins with a dataset constructed at the level of unique measurement values, with associated frequencies, age midpoints, and gender. Each row represents a value-frequency pair within a demographic context, and all methods operate on this shared input structure. The first stage consists of applying a set of univariate outlier detection methods to the full distribution of measurement values. These methods include Tukey fences, quantile-based thresholds, Z-scores, modified Z-scores based on median absolute deviation, and the generalized extreme Studentized deviate (ESD) test. Each method produces a binary indicator identifying whether a given value is considered an outlier under its assumptions. From these outputs, method-specific plausible ranges can be derived by retaining values not flagged as outliers and computing lower and upper bounds using either minimum and maximum values or robust quantiles.

In parallel, the same univariate methods are applied within strata defined by age bands and gender, provided sufficient data are available within each stratum. Age bands are selected to reflect clinically meaningful groupings (e.g., 18–39, 40–64, 65–79, 80+ for adult populations), balancing biological relevance with statistical stability. This stratified analysis allows estimation of context-specific plausible ranges and enables comparison with the global ranges derived from the full dataset. The stratified results provide insight into whether the distribution of measurement values varies meaningfully across demographic subgroups and whether global thresholds obscure important differences.

To complement the univariate methods, two context-aware anomaly detection approaches are applied: Gaussian anomaly detection (GAD) and Isolation Forest (IF). These methods incorporate measurement value, age midpoint, and gender (and frequency, in the case of Isolation Forest) as input features. Both methods produce binary outlier indicators at the row level, allowing direct comparison with the univariate methods. Unlike the univariate approaches, which primarily assess marginal deviations in value, GAD and IF evaluate whether a value is unusual given its demographic context and overall distributional structure. These methods are not used to directly define plausible ranges but instead to identify values that may be implausible in context even if they fall within global or stratified statistical bounds.

All methods are harmonized by representing their outputs as binary outlier flags on the same dataset. This enables construction of consensus measures, such as the number of methods flagging each value as an outlier, separately for global univariate methods, stratified univariate methods, and context-aware models. Each value can then be classified into categories based on agreement across methods, including consistently plausible values, globally implausible values, context-dependent anomalies, and borderline cases with partial disagreement. Final plausible ranges are derived by retaining values not flagged by a predefined consensus threshold among the global univariate methods, with stratified results and ML-based flags used to refine interpretation and assess the robustness of the selected bounds.

## Anticipated Results

The consensus of global univariate methods is expected to produce a stable and interpretable plausible range that reflects the overall distribution of measurement values while reducing sensitivity to the assumptions of any single method. Compared to individual methods, which may produce overly narrow or overly wide bounds depending on distributional characteristics, the consensus-based approach is anticipated to yield a balanced range that excludes clearly implausible values while retaining the majority of valid observations.

Stratified analyses are expected to reveal meaningful differences in plausible ranges across age and gender groups, particularly for measurements such as systolic blood pressure that vary systematically with age. In such cases, global bounds may obscure clinically relevant variation, whereas stratified bounds provide a more accurate representation of biologically plausible values within subpopulations. However, stratification may also introduce instability in smaller strata, highlighting the need to balance granularity with data sufficiency.

Gaussian anomaly detection is expected to provide a useful baseline for identifying extreme values under parametric assumptions but may be sensitive to skewness and heavy tails in real-world data. Isolation Forest is anticipated to identify additional anomalies that are not captured by univariate methods, particularly values that are rare or structurally unusual given their demographic context. These context-aware methods are expected to flag values that fall within global statistical bounds but are atypical for specific age or gender groups, thereby providing complementary information to the univariate analyses.

Overall, the combined framework is expected to improve the differentiation between clearly implausible values, rare but valid observations, and context-dependent anomalies. The final output will include both a global plausible range and, where supported by the data, stratified ranges that better reflect biological variation. In addition, the classification of values based on agreement across methods will provide a transparent and reproducible basis for data quality assessment and downstream decision-making. This approach is intended to support functional implementation within analytical pipelines and to serve as a foundation for integration into data quality tools such as the OHDSI DataQualityDashboard.
